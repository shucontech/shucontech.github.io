The admission process in a hospital is the step-by-step procedure that converts a doctor's admission advice into an admitted inpatient: estimate and deposit or insurance approval, registration, consent, bed allocation, patient identification and handover to the ward, with an inpatient billing account opened. IPD stands for In-Patient Department, so the IPD admission process covers every patient who will occupy a bed. Done well, admission is quick for the family and leaves billing, nursing and the insurance desk with correct information from the first hour.

Admission is often treated as a front-desk formality. In practice it sets up most of what follows: the tariff the patient is billed on, whether the payer will approve the stay, which doctor is responsible and how fast the patient reaches a bed. Errors made at admission tend to surface days later, at the billing counter or in a claim query, when they are harder and more expensive to fix.

IPD full form and types of admission

IPD full form: In-Patient Department. Its counterpart is OPD, the Out-Patient Department, where patients are seen and leave the same day. Hospitals usually handle three kinds of admission, and the procedure differs for each:

Admission typeTypical triggerWhat changes in the process
Planned (elective)OPD consultation, scheduled surgeryEstimate, pre-authorisation and bed booking can be done in advance
EmergencyCasualty or emergency departmentTreatment first; registration, consent and payer checks completed once safe
Day careShort procedures such as chemotherapy cycles or minor surgeryShorter stay but still needs consent, billing account and discharge

Many planned admissions start in the OPD. The share of OPD patients who are advised and actually admitted is tracked as the OPD to IPD conversion ratio, and a slow or confusing admission process is one of the reasons families choose another hospital.

Admission process in hospital step by step

The sequence below is a typical planned IPD admission procedure. Emergency admissions follow the same steps, but some of them are completed after treatment has started.

  1. Admission advice: the consultant records the reason for admission, provisional diagnosis, planned treatment or procedure, expected length of stay and preferred ward or room category.
  2. Estimate and counselling: the admission desk explains the expected cost by category, room rent, likely exclusions and the deposit or patient-payable amount.
  3. Payer verification: the desk confirms whether the patient is cash, insured through a TPA or insurer, corporate, or covered by a government scheme, and starts the right approval path.
  4. Deposit or approval: cash patients pay the agreed advance; cashless patients move through pre-authorisation; corporate and scheme patients follow their own eligibility checks.
  5. Registration: the patient's existing UHID is linked or a new one is created, and an IP number is generated for this admission with the admitting consultant, department and payer.
  6. Consent: general admission consent and financial consent are signed; procedure-specific and anaesthesia consents are taken by the clinical team at the right time.
  7. Bed allocation: bed management assigns a bed in the agreed category and confirms it is clean and ready.
  8. Identification: the patient receives an ID wristband with the identifiers your hospital uses, such as name, UHID or IP number and date of birth.
  9. Handover to ward: the patient is escorted to the ward with the file, admission orders and any pending tests; nursing completes the initial assessment.
  10. Billing account opening: the IP account is opened on the correct tariff, package and payer so every charge from the first hour posts correctly.

In a connected hospital management system, steps 3 to 10 share one record, so the ward, billing and insurance desk see the same payer, tariff and consultant without re-entry.

Step details that matter most

Estimate and deposit

An estimate is an explanation, not a promise. It should show room rent, the expected procedure or package, likely investigations, pharmacy and consumables, items a payer may not cover and the deposit. Families who understand the estimate at admission argue less at discharge. Hospitals should define who may approve a lower deposit and record the reason, because informal deposit waivers are hard to recover later.

Payer verification: cash, TPA, corporate or scheme

Payer type decides the tariff, the documents and who pays. For insured patients, the TPA desk should check policy status, room rent eligibility and co-pay before the bed is allotted, so the patient is not admitted to a category the policy will not cover in full. The request itself follows the pre-authorisation process. Government scheme and corporate patients need their own eligibility checks under current scheme rules and agreements.

Registration and consent

Registration errors travel. A misspelt name or wrong age on the IP record can cause a mismatch with the insurance card, which then shows up as a claim query. Consents should be complete, signed by the right person and stored where the ward and the medical records department can find them. Specific consent requirements should follow your hospital's policies, accreditation standards and legal advice.

Bed allocation and handover

Bed allocation is where admission meets discharge. If beds are released late, admissions queue in the lobby or emergency. Tracking discharge TAT and bed occupancy together shows whether the admission delay is really a discharge problem.

How to measure admission TAT

Admission turnaround time is easiest to manage when it is broken into timestamped segments. The worked example below is illustrative.

EventTimeSegment duration
Admission advice recorded10:40–
Estimate explained, deposit paid11:0525 min
Registration and consent completed11:2015 min
Bed allocated and ready12:1050 min
Patient received in ward12:2515 min
Admission TAT = time patient received in ward − time admission advice recorded = 12:25 − 10:40 = 1 hour 45 minutes

Here, 50 of the 105 minutes were spent waiting for a bed. Speeding up the registration counter would barely change the total; the fix is earlier discharge planning and faster bed cleaning. Without segment timestamps, the front desk would likely be blamed for a delay it does not own.

Common admission delays

  • No bed ready because discharges are completed late in the day.
  • Payer verification started only after the patient reaches the ward.
  • Estimate not prepared in advance for planned admissions.
  • Deposit discussions with no clear approval rule for exceptions.
  • Duplicate data entry between admission, ward and billing systems.
  • Admission orders not ready, so the ward cannot start care.

Revenue leakage points at admission

Admission is also where several revenue leaks begin. The common ones:

  • Wrong tariff or room category: the patient occupies one category but the account is opened on another.
  • Wrong payer: an insured patient is registered as cash, or the reverse, so approvals and tariffs do not match.
  • Package not attached: a package patient is billed itemised, or extra items are not tracked against package limits.
  • Deposit not collected or waived informally: the hospital carries credit risk it never approved.
  • Room transfers not updated: ICU or room changes are not reflected in bed charges.

A short illustrative example: a patient stays four days in a twin-sharing room billed at ₹4,000 per day, but the account was opened on the general ward rate of ₹2,500. The shortfall is (₹4,000 − ₹2,500) × 4 = ₹6,000, and nobody notices unless someone compares the bed census with the billing account. These checks sit naturally in the hospital billing process and the hospital revenue leakage checklist.

Fix the account on day one

Correcting a tariff, payer or package on the day of admission takes minutes. Correcting it at discharge means re-billing, re-approval and an unhappy family.

IPD admission checklist

A one-page checklist at the admission desk keeps the process consistent across shifts:

  • Admission advice with diagnosis, planned treatment and expected stay.
  • Estimate explained and signed; deposit collected or exception approved.
  • Payer type confirmed; pre-authorisation submitted where applicable.
  • UHID linked, IP number created, consultant and department correct.
  • General and financial consent signed.
  • Bed category matches estimate and payer eligibility.
  • ID wristband applied and checked.
  • File, orders and pending tests handed over to ward nursing.
  • Billing account open on correct tariff, package and payer.
