A discharge summary format is the standard layout a hospital uses to record a patient's stay at discharge: identifiers, admission and discharge dates, diagnosis, procedures, course in hospital, key investigations, condition at discharge, medicines, follow-up advice and the treating doctor's signature. A good format makes the summary useful to the patient's next doctor and complete enough for insurers and TPAs to approve the claim without queries.

The discharge summary is one of the most read documents a hospital produces. The patient takes it home, the family doctor reads it, the insurance desk attaches it to the claim and it may be reviewed months later in an audit. Yet it is often written in a hurry at the end of the stay, by a resident who is also managing the ward. A consistent format turns it into a checklist rather than a blank page.

What a discharge summary must contain

The exact headings depend on your hospital's policy, specialty and accreditation standards, so treat the list below as good practice rather than a regulatory template. Most well-designed discharge summaries cover:

  • Patient identifiers: full name, age, sex, UHID, IP number and contact details.
  • Admission details: date and time of admission and discharge, ward or room, treating consultant and department.
  • Type of discharge: routine discharge, transfer to another facility, discharge against medical advice or death summary.
  • Diagnosis: provisional diagnosis at admission and final diagnosis, with codes if your hospital or payer uses them.
  • Presenting complaints and history: symptoms with duration, relevant past history, comorbidities and allergies.
  • Examination findings at admission: key vitals and relevant findings.
  • Procedures and surgeries: name, date, surgeon, anaesthesia type and implants used.
  • Course in hospital: a short narrative of treatment, response and any complications or ICU stay.
  • Key investigations: the results that support the diagnosis and treatment.
  • Condition at discharge: clinical status and vitals at the time of discharge.
  • Discharge medicines: drug, dose, route, frequency and duration.
  • Advice and follow-up: diet, activity, wound care, warning signs and follow-up date.
  • Authentication: name, signature and registration number of the treating doctor, with date.

Discharge summary format for insurance claim

For insured patients, the discharge summary is a core claim document for both cashless final approval and reimbursement. Insurers and TPAs commonly read it alongside the pre-authorisation, the final bill and the investigation reports. In practice, they tend to check:

  1. Identity match: name, age and sex match the policy, ID and bill.
  2. Date match: admission and discharge dates and times match the bill and the room charges.
  3. Diagnosis consistency: the final diagnosis matches or explains any change from the pre-authorised diagnosis.
  4. History and duration: how long the condition and comorbidities have existed, which payers may use to assess policy conditions such as waiting periods.
  5. Justification for admission: findings and investigations that support the need for inpatient care.
  6. Procedure details: name, date and implants match the OT notes and bill.
  7. Line of treatment: medical or surgical management described clearly enough to match billed items.
  8. Authentication: signature, doctor's name and registration number, and hospital seal if your payers ask for it.

The TPA desk should review every insured discharge summary against the pre-authorisation and the bill before it is sent. That review takes minutes and can prevent a query that holds the file for days.

One story across three documents

The pre-authorisation, the bill and the discharge summary should tell the same story: the same patient, dates, diagnosis and procedure. Most avoidable claim queries come from a mismatch between them.

Common discharge summary errors that cause claim queries

  • Date or time mismatch: the summary says discharge on one date and the bill charges room rent for another.
  • Changed diagnosis without explanation: the pre-authorisation said one condition, the summary says another, and the course in hospital does not explain why.
  • Missing duration of illness or comorbidities: the payer cannot assess policy conditions and raises a query.
  • Vague procedure details: "surgery done" instead of the procedure name, date and implants.
  • Copy-paste text: sections carried over from another patient or an older admission.
  • Unexplained abbreviations: local shorthand that a reviewer cannot interpret.
  • Missing signature or registration number: the document is not properly authenticated.
  • Contradiction with case sheet: the summary says uneventful stay while notes record an ICU transfer that is also billed.

Errors like these show up in TPA claim rejection reasons and claim deductions in hospital bills. Fixing them at the source is cheaper than replying to queries after discharge.

Who prepares it and when

The resident or medical officer usually drafts the summary and the treating consultant reviews and signs it. The biggest improvement most hospitals can make is timing: start the draft as soon as discharge is likely, not after the final order. Predictable cases, such as planned surgeries, can have most sections filled in the day before. This is one of the most effective ways to reduce discharge TAT, because the summary is often the first item in the discharge chain.

A simple rule set helps:

  • Draft summary started when the patient is marked likely for discharge.
  • Investigations and procedure details pulled from the system, not retyped.
  • Consultant review and signature before the final bill is frozen.
  • Insurance desk check for insured patients before submission.
  • Copy given to the patient and stored in the medical record.

Worked example: one query avoided

This example is illustrative. A patient is pre-authorised for medical management of a condition, but on day two the team performs a procedure. The bill includes the procedure, the OT charges and the implant. If the discharge summary only lists the original diagnosis and says "managed conservatively", the payer sees a bill that does not match the clinical story and raises a query. If the summary records why the plan changed, the procedure name and date, and the implant used, and the desk has raised an enhancement with the same explanation, the final approval can proceed on the documents already submitted.
