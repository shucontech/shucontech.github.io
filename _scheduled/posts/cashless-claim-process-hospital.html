The cashless claim process in a hospital is the step-by-step workflow where the hospital verifies insurance, sends pre-authorisation, treats the patient, submits discharge documents and receives eligible payment directly from the insurer or TPA. The patient may still pay deposits, co-pay, non-payable items or disallowed amounts depending on the policy, package and current payer rules.

For hospital TPA desks, the cashless process is a daily coordination exercise between patient, doctor, billing, nursing, diagnostics, insurer and accounts. A smooth claim protects patient experience, discharge TAT and cash flow. A weak process creates approval delays, billing arguments, deductions and ageing receivables. The steps below are written for Indian hospitals, but the operating principles also apply to hospital groups and clinics in the UAE that manage payer approvals.

Cashless hospitalisation process at a glance

The cashless workflow has three broad phases: admission approval, treatment monitoring and final claim submission. Each phase needs clear documentation and ownership. Do not treat cashless as only a TPA desk job; the TPA desk depends on clinical notes, investigation reports, billing accuracy and discharge summaries from multiple departments.

Most hospitals already know these stages. The difference between an average TPA desk and a strong one is whether every file has a next action, deadline and owner. That operating discipline also improves the claims section of a hospital KPI dashboard over time.

Step 1: verify insurance eligibility and policy details

At admission or planned admission, collect the patient identity proof, insurance card or policy details, corporate card if applicable and any previous medical papers. The TPA desk should verify active policy status, room eligibility, waiting periods where relevant, co-pay, exclusions, corporate approval rules and whether the hospital is networked for that payer. Because insurer and IRDAI-linked processes may change, hospitals should check current official and payer guidance instead of relying on old SOP copies.

  • Match patient name, age and policy number carefully.
  • Confirm whether admission is emergency or planned.
  • Capture diagnosis, planned procedure and expected length of stay.
  • Explain likely non-payable items and deposits clearly to the patient family.

Clear counselling at this stage prevents discharge conflict later. Patients often hear "cashless" as "no payment at all"; the hospital must explain that cashless usually covers approved eligible amounts, not every line item.

Step 2: submit pre-authorisation for cashless approval

Pre-authorisation is the insurer or TPA approval before treatment or soon after emergency admission. The hospital sends clinical details, estimated cost and supporting documents through the prescribed channel. This stage should be reviewed with the same discipline used for finance and operations KPIs.

Pre-authorisation TAT = Approval or query time - complete request submission time

Example: a planned orthopaedic surgery has an estimated package of ₹2.4 lakh. The TPA desk submits the request at 10:30 am with diagnosis, X-ray report, surgeon note, policy details and estimate. The payer asks for implant details at 12:00 pm. If the OT coordinator replies at 4:00 pm, four hours are lost inside the hospital, not at the payer. Measuring only payer TAT hides the internal delay.

Separate payer delay from hospital delay

Track every query with timestamp, department owner and pending document. This avoids blaming the insurer for delays caused by missing hospital inputs.

Step 3: monitor approvals during the patient stay

Approval at admission is not the end of the claim. Diagnosis can change, procedure scope can expand, implants may differ, ICU stay may extend and consumables may exceed the initial estimate. The TPA desk should review high-value cashless patients daily with billing and the clinical team.

  1. Compare approved amount with running bill.
  2. Trigger enhancement request before the bill crosses approval.
  3. Keep investigation reports, OT notes and progress notes ready.
  4. Flag exclusions and non-payables to the billing counsellor early.

If a patient is approved for ₹1.8 lakh but the running bill reaches ₹2.35 lakh because of two extra ICU days, the enhancement should not wait until discharge morning. Late enhancement is one of the common reasons discharge counters become crowded and emotional.

Step 4: prepare discharge and final approval

On discharge day, the final bill, discharge summary, prescriptions, reports, implant stickers where relevant, pharmacy returns, OT notes and payment breakup must align. A mismatch between clinical summary and billed items invites query or deduction. This is where hospitals should connect the cashless workflow with broader charge capture controls.

A practical discharge checklist for cashless patients should include:

  • Discharge summary completed and signed as per hospital policy.
  • Final diagnosis and procedure match pre-authorisation or enhancement notes.
  • Pharmacy returns posted before final bill lock.
  • Non-payable items separated and explained to patient family.
  • Final approval received before patient exit wherever payer rules require it.

Example: a hospital final bill is ₹3.1 lakh. The insurer approves ₹2.72 lakh, excludes ₹18,000 as non-payable consumables and applies ₹20,000 co-pay. If the family was counselled only for the initial approval of ₹2.5 lakh, the final discussion becomes difficult. If counselling was updated daily, the same numbers are manageable.

Step 5: submit claim file and track receivables

After discharge, the hospital must submit the claim file with required documents in the prescribed format and timeline. Requirements differ by payer, corporate agreement and scheme, so the TPA desk should maintain payer-wise checklists that are reviewed periodically. Missing signatures, unclear scans, incomplete bills and absent reports create avoidable ageing.

Track claim status in ageing buckets: 0 to 7 days, 8 to 15 days, 16 to 30 days, 31 to 60 days and above 60 days. CFOs should review value, not only count. Ten small pending files may matter less than one ₹18 lakh cardiac file pending for want of an operative note.

Settlement posting should be as disciplined as submission. When payment arrives, accounts should post the approved amount, deduction, patient recovery and write-off reason separately. If a ₹6.4 lakh file settles at ₹5.92 lakh, the ₹48,000 gap should not disappear inside a generic adjustment. It should show whether the reason was policy exclusion, late document, package disagreement or hospital-approved concession.

Common cashless claim rejection and deduction reasons

Cashless claims do not fail only because of payer behaviour. Many deductions are triggered by documentation gaps, package mismatch, room category eligibility, non-payable items, late intimation, missing pre-authorisation, unclear medical necessity or inconsistent coding. Link this review to discount and refund controls so approved write-offs are not mixed with avoidable deductions.

  • Clinical mismatch: diagnosis in summary does not support procedure billed.
  • Documentation gap: report, note, consent or sticker missing.
  • Policy limitation: co-pay, sub-limit, waiting period or exclusion applies.
  • Billing mismatch: package inclusion billed separately without approval.

Hedge all patient communication carefully. The hospital can explain what is usually payable or non-payable, but the final decision may rest with the insurer, TPA, corporate payer or scheme authority under current policy terms.

Cashless claim SOP and dashboard for hospitals

A hospital cashless SOP should define who captures documents, who submits pre-authorisation, who replies to queries, who requests enhancement, who approves patient communication and who follows pending claims. The dashboard should show pending admission approvals, discharge approvals, query ageing, claim submission ageing, payer-wise AR and deduction value.

Technology helps when it reduces manual chasing. Insurance claim agents can watch pending documents, payer queries and ageing buckets, then create worklists for the TPA desk and billing team. Shucon MedAI should be used as an operating layer that helps teams move claims faster; it should not be positioned as a substitute for checking policy terms, IRDAI updates or payer-specific instructions.

Finally, connect claims to revenue leadership. A cashless process is not complete when the patient leaves; it is complete when the hospital receives the approved money, posts deductions correctly and learns from the reason. Linking this workflow to hospital revenue leakage reviews gives the CFO a cleaner view of cash, risk and process accountability.
