AR days hospital is a finance metric that shows how many days of average credit revenue are tied up in unpaid receivables. Lower AR days usually means the hospital is converting approved bills into cash faster, while higher AR days signals delayed claims, payer queries, deductions or weak follow-up. For Indian hospitals with insurers, TPAs, corporates and scheme payments, AR days should be tracked by payer category rather than as one blended number only.

What AR days mean in hospital finance

Accounts receivable, or AR, is money billed but not yet collected. In hospitals, this may include insurer claims awaiting settlement, TPA approvals pending payment, corporate credit bills, government scheme dues and sometimes patient balances. AR days converts the outstanding amount into a time measure so finance can ask: how many days of normal credit revenue is stuck outside the bank?

A rising AR balance is not always bad if revenue is growing. That is why AR days is more useful than outstanding amount alone. ₹2 crore outstanding may be manageable for a large hospital with high daily credit billing, but serious for a smaller hospital. The metric should be read with payer mix, claim quality and deduction trends.

AR days hospital formula

The basic calculation is straightforward, but definitions matter. Use net credit revenue, not gross billing, and include only receivables related to the same payer scope. If you calculate insurance AR days, use insurance receivables and average daily insurance revenue. If you calculate overall credit AR days, include all credit payers and their related net revenue.

AR days = accounts receivable balance ÷ average daily net credit revenue.

Average daily net credit revenue is usually calculated as net credit revenue for the period divided by the number of days in that period. Some hospitals use trailing three months to smooth seasonality. Whichever method is chosen, keep it consistent so trends are meaningful.

Match numerator and denominator

Do not divide TPA receivables by total hospital revenue including cash patients. That makes AR days look better than the collection reality.

Worked INR example for hospital AR days

Suppose a hospital has ₹1.80 crore in outstanding insurance and corporate receivables at month end. Over the last 30 days, it generated ₹2.40 crore in net credit revenue from the same payer group. Average daily net credit revenue is ₹2.40 crore divided by 30, which is ₹8 lakh per day. AR days are therefore ₹1.80 crore divided by ₹8 lakh, or 22.5 days.

Why AR days increase in hospitals

High AR days usually come from operational causes. Claims may be submitted late because discharge documents are incomplete. Payers may raise queries because clinical notes are unclear. Bills may exceed pre-authorised amounts without timely enhancement. Deductions may sit unresolved. Receipts may be collected but not posted correctly. Old corporate accounts may not have named owners.

  • Delayed submission: final bill is ready, but claim file lacks reports or signatures.
  • Query backlog: TPA questions wait with ward, doctor or insurance desk.
  • Deduction disputes: short payments are not coded or escalated.
  • Weak reconciliation: bank receipts and claim settlements are posted late.
  • No ageing ownership: every file is pending, but nobody owns the next action.

Many causes begin earlier in the hospital revenue cycle management India workflow. AR follow-up cannot fully compensate for poor charge capture, weak pre-auth or incomplete documentation.

AR ageing and payer-wise follow-up

Ageing buckets help teams prioritise. A single outstanding list sorted by amount is not enough. Hospitals should view receivables by payer, age, claim status, query reason, owner and promised payment date. A ₹3 lakh file pending for 95 days with no owner may be more urgent than a ₹9 lakh file submitted yesterday.

Useful AR review fields include patient ID, payer, admission and discharge date, bill value, submitted value, approved value, outstanding amount, last action date, pending reason and next owner. For cashless cases, include pre-auth and enhancement references. For corporate accounts, include invoice submission date and purchase order or agreement reference where applicable.

How claim quality affects AR days

Clean claims are paid faster because the payer has fewer reasons to ask questions. If claims are repeatedly missing discharge summaries, operative notes, investigation reports or approval references, AR days rise even when the billing team follows up daily. The pre-authorisation quality should therefore be reviewed alongside AR days.

Deductions also affect AR. A short payment may remain open for weeks if the hospital does not decide whether to dispute, recover from patient or write off. The claim deductions in hospital bill process should define reason codes and authority levels so finance is not left with unresolved balances.

Receivable follow-up quality = every ageing item has a reason, owner, last action date and next action date.

Practical ways to reduce AR days

  1. Submit faster: set a target for claim submission after discharge and track misses by reason.
  2. Improve pre-auth quality: reduce approval gaps before the final bill is submitted.
  3. Use payer-wise ageing: separate insurer, TPA, corporate and scheme receivables.
  4. Assign ownership: every query should have a named hospital owner and due date.
  5. Code deductions: separate recoverable, disputed and write-off amounts.
  6. Post receipts quickly: reconcile bank credits, remittance advice and patient ledgers.
  7. Escalate old files: review 60+ and 90+ day accounts with leadership weekly.

Hospitals can also link AR review to discharge billing controls. A file delayed at discharge often becomes a file delayed in collection. The hospital billing process lens helps identify document and approval delays before they turn into receivable ageing.

Separate old AR from active AR

Hospitals often mix recently submitted claims with old disputed accounts in the same review. This makes the team look busy but does not clear the difficult backlog. Create separate reviews for active AR, where payment is expected through normal follow-up, and old AR, where a decision is needed on dispute, escalation, patient recovery, write-off or legal route. The authority level should match the age and amount.

Old AR should not be allowed to survive only because no one wants to close it. If the hospital has no document, no payer acknowledgement and no realistic recovery path, finance should follow its approved provisioning and write-off policy. If recovery is possible, the next action must be specific: upload a document, meet the corporate desk, reconcile a remittance advice or escalate to payer management.

Daily habits that protect monthly cash

AR days improve through daily habits, not only month-end pressure. Submit yesterday's discharge files, close yesterday's payer queries, post yesterday's receipts and review yesterday's short payments. A ten-minute daily huddle between insurance, billing and finance can prevent ageing growth by making pending ownership visible before files become old.

Using technology to keep AR moving

AR days reduce when the next action is visible. Dashboards should not only show outstanding balances; they should show why each file is pending and who owns it. Insurance desk, billing, doctors and finance need a shared queue for documents, queries, approvals, deductions and payment follow-up. Automated reminders help only when the underlying status is accurate.
