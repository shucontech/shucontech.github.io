Pharmacy expiry management is the process of tracking medicine expiry dates batch-wise and acting before stock becomes unsellable, unusable or unsafe. The simplest rule is FEFO: First Expiry, First Out, supported by near-expiry alerts, slow-moving stock review and vendor return controls. In a hospital pharmacy, expiry is not a back-office inconvenience; it directly affects cash, compliance and patient safety.

Hospitals often discover expiry loss only during stock audit, when the medicine is already dead stock. By then, the pharmacy head can only segregate, write off, return if permitted, or dispose according to policy. A better system flags expiry risk while there is still time to consume, transfer, return or stop reordering.

Why pharmacy expiry management matters

Hospital pharmacies carry a difficult mix: routine fast-moving medicines, emergency drugs, high-value injectables, implants, contrast media, vaccines, cold-chain items and specialty stock requested by consultants. Some items move daily. Others sit because the case mix changed or a doctor stopped prescribing them. Without batch-level control, the pharmacy may look well stocked while value is quietly ageing toward expiry.

Expiry control also connects with pharmacy inventory reconciliation. If stock records do not match physical stock, expiry reports become unreliable. A medicine that appears available in the system may not be present on the shelf, while a near-expiry batch may be hidden in a ward sub-store.

FEFO pharmacy process explained

FEFO means the batch expiring first should be issued first. It is more appropriate for medicines than FIFO because purchase date is less important than expiry date. A later-purchased batch may expire earlier than older stock, especially when vendors supply mixed batches.

FEFO issue priority = Earliest valid expiry batch available in the correct location, subject to clinical suitability and storage condition

FEFO fails when batch capture is weak, shelves are not arranged by expiry, or the software allows staff to pick any batch without warning. It also fails when ward stock and emergency trays are replenished without rotating older batches back into the main flow.

FEFO needs physical discipline

A software alert cannot help if near-expiry stock is physically behind fresh stock. Shelf arrangement, bin labels and batch scanning must support the rule.

Near-expiry report: what it should include

A useful expiry report is not just a list of dates. It should help the pharmacy head decide what to do next. The report should show value, location and movement speed so the team can separate urgent action from normal monitoring.

Many hospitals start with 90-day, 60-day and 30-day buckets. High-value injectables and cold-chain items may need earlier alerts. The exact thresholds should be based on purchase terms, consumption rate and clinical criticality.

The report should also separate usable near-expiry stock from blocked stock. For example, a batch held due to temperature excursion, damaged strip, recall communication or quality complaint should not be treated as available simply because its expiry date is still valid. Pharmacy heads should review these exception buckets separately so the purchase team does not rely on stock that cannot be dispensed.

Worked example: ₹4.2 lakh near-expiry risk

Assume a 150-bed hospital pharmacy runs a monthly expiry report. It finds three high-value items approaching expiry within 90 days: 40 vials of an injectable antibiotic worth ₹3,200 each, 12 contrast media units worth ₹6,500 each and 18 specialty injections worth ₹12,000 each. The total at-risk value is:

(40 × ₹3,200) + (12 × ₹6,500) + (18 × ₹12,000) = ₹4.22 lakh

The antibiotic moves regularly, so the pharmacy shifts the earliest batch to the primary dispensing shelf and stops new purchase for two weeks. Contrast media consumption is linked to radiology schedule, so the pharmacy coordinates with diagnostics and transfers six units to a busier branch. The specialty injection has almost no recent consumption, so the purchase team checks vendor return terms. Even if only half the value is saved, the hospital avoids a ₹2 lakh write-off.

The lesson is that expiry control is not just reporting. It needs ownership, inter-department coordination and purchase discipline.

Expiry management SOP for hospital pharmacy

A practical SOP should define who reviews near-expiry stock, how often, and what actions are allowed. It should also define segregation of expired stock so it is not accidentally dispensed. Hospitals should check current drug rules and local guidance for storage, return and disposal requirements, especially for controlled, cold-chain or high-risk items.

  1. Capture batch number and expiry date at purchase inward.
  2. Verify physical quantity, invoice quantity and system entry before stock is accepted.
  3. Arrange shelves by FEFO and mark near-expiry bins clearly.
  4. Run near-expiry reports weekly or monthly based on volume.
  5. Review slow-moving and non-moving items before placing fresh purchase orders.
  6. Transfer stock between pharmacy, wards and branches where clinically appropriate.
  7. Segregate expired stock immediately and process return or disposal as per policy.

This process should be reviewed alongside pharmacy stock reconciliation, because weak controls around stock movement can create both expiry loss and pharmacy pilferage risk.

Purchase controls that prevent expiry write-offs

Expiry loss often begins at purchase. A pharmacy may buy too much because discounts look attractive, because minimum order quantities are high, or because departments request safety stock without reviewing consumption. Bulk buying is sensible only when consumption is predictable and shelf life is sufficient.

  • Use consumption-based reorder levels. Do not let reorder quantities ignore recent movement.
  • Check remaining shelf life at inward. Define minimum acceptable shelf life by item category.
  • Review substitutes. Multiple brands of the same molecule can split consumption and increase expiry risk.
  • Link purchase to formulary decisions. If consultants change preference, purchase masters must change too.
  • Track vendor return terms. Returnable and non-returnable items should be treated differently in buying decisions.

ABC VED classification helps here because high-value and critical medicines need different controls. A hospital can combine expiry reports with ABC VED analysis to prioritise management attention.

Ward stock, emergency trays and hidden expiry

Expiry risk is not limited to the main pharmacy. Wards, ICU, OT, emergency, crash carts and diagnostic areas often hold stock outside the main counter. These locations are necessary for patient care, but they create blind spots if not counted and rotated.

Every ward stock location should have a defined owner, maximum quantity, minimum quantity and review schedule. Near-expiry medicines in low-consumption wards should be rotated back to the main pharmacy early. Emergency trays should be checked after every use and at fixed intervals. Cold-chain items need temperature governance in addition to expiry tracking.

Administrators should also watch inter-location transfers. A transfer should reduce risk, not simply move near-expiry stock from the main pharmacy to a ward where nobody notices it. Each transfer should have a reason, receiving owner and expected consumption plan. If that plan is absent, the transfer should be treated as deferred write-off risk.

How software reduces pharmacy expiry loss

A connected pharmacy system should capture batch and expiry at inward, enforce FEFO at issue, show near-expiry value by location, and block expired stock from dispensing. It should also connect purchase, pharmacy, wards and billing so stock movement is visible rather than dependent on manual registers.
