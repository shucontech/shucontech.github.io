A hospital pharmacy audit checklist is a structured list of checks that confirms the pharmacy's physical stock, system records, dispensing, billing, storage and documentation all agree with each other. Run as an internal audit every month, it catches stock variances, expired medicines, unbilled dispensing and register gaps before they become losses or inspection findings.

Hospital pharmacies handle thousands of items, multiple batches per item, ward issues, returns and emergency dispensing every day. Small errors accumulate quietly: a return not updated, a strip converted incorrectly, an injection issued in an emergency and never billed. A pharmacy stock audit brings those errors to the surface while they are still small and traceable.

Types of pharmacy audit

A complete hospital pharmacy audit combines three views of the same stock:

Audit typeQuestion it answersTypical frequency
Stock auditDoes physical stock match the system, item by item and batch by batch?Full count periodically; cycle counts for high-risk items
Dispensing vs billing auditDid every item that left the pharmacy reach a patient bill or an approved cost centre?Daily exception review, monthly sample audit
Compliance and storage auditAre registers, storage, labelling and access in line with policy and applicable law?Monthly internal check, before external inspections

A general stores stock audit focuses on quantities and value. A pharmacy audit adds batch, expiry, controlled drugs, cold chain and patient billing, which is why it needs its own checklist.

Before the audit

  • Fix the audit date and cut-off time; freeze or log all movements during the count.
  • Post all pending receipts, issues, returns and transfers before the cut-off.
  • Print or export the system stock by item, batch and expiry for the locations being audited.
  • Use counters who are not responsible for that store's daily stock, where possible.
  • Prefer blind counts, where counters record what they find without seeing system quantities.
  • Agree how to treat loose units, partial strips and opened multi-dose items.

Pharmacy audit checklist

1. Stock vs system

  • Physical count matches system quantity for each item and batch.
  • Units of measure are consistent: strips, tablets, vials and packs are not mixed up.
  • Stock in satellite locations (ICU, OT, emergency, ward cupboards) is counted and recorded against the right location.
  • Negative stock or items with system stock but no physical location are investigated.
  • Manual stock adjustments since the last audit have a reason and an approver.

2. Batch and expiry

  • Batch numbers and expiry dates on the shelf match the system.
  • Expired stock is segregated, labelled and recorded, not mixed with saleable stock.
  • Near-expiry items are listed for transfer, return to supplier or priority use.
  • Stock is issued first-expiry-first-out, and older batches are not hidden behind newer ones.

Expiry control is covered in more depth in expiry management in hospital pharmacy.

3. High-value and controlled drugs

  • Controlled, narcotic and psychotropic drugs are counted in full, not sampled.
  • Registers required under applicable drug laws and your licence conditions are complete, up to date and signed.
  • Each issue is linked to a prescription and a patient, and balances in the register match physical stock.
  • Storage is locked, with keys or access limited to authorised staff.
  • High-value injectables, oncology drugs and implants are counted in full and matched to patient bills.

Specific register formats and storage rules depend on the drug category and the law that applies, so check them with your pharmacist-in-charge and current regulatory guidance.

4. Returns

  • Ward and patient returns are recorded with batch, expiry, quantity and the original bill.
  • Returned items are credited to the patient account and added back to stock on the same day.
  • Returns to suppliers have debit notes and are reflected in stock and accounts.
  • Returned items that cannot be reused are written off with a reason.

5. Dispensing vs billing reconciliation

  • Every IP issue maps to a patient bill or an approved departmental cost centre.
  • Emergency issues without a bill are listed and closed within an agreed time.
  • Cancelled or modified pharmacy bills after dispensing have a reason and approver.
  • Package patients' medicine use is tracked against package inclusions.
  • Free or discounted issues follow the approved discount policy.

This is the core of pharmacy inventory reconciliation and the main defence against pharmacy pilferage.

6. Storage and cold chain

  • Refrigerated items are stored within the label-specified range, often 2–8°C, with temperature logs completed.
  • Temperature excursions are recorded with action taken.
  • Room temperature, humidity and protection from light follow labels for sensitive items.
  • Look-alike and sound-alike medicines are stored apart and labelled clearly.
  • Shelves are clean, labelled and organised so counts and picking are reliable.

7. Documentation and access

  • Licences and registrations are valid and displayed where required.
  • Purchase invoices match goods received notes and system receipts.
  • User access in the pharmacy software matches current staff and roles.
  • Price changes and item master edits are logged with the user and approver.
  • Previous audit findings have documented corrective actions.

Audit for inspection readiness, not just numbers

A pharmacy inspection checklist from a regulator or accreditation body will focus on registers, storage, licences and safe practice. Running the compliance sections of this checklist every month means inspection preparation is routine rather than a scramble.

How to calculate stock variance

Stock variance (quantity) = system quantity − physical quantity
Variance value = variance quantity × unit cost
Unexplained variance = variance value − value of documented pending adjustments

Here is an illustrative example. An injection shows a system stock of 240 vials and a physical count of 228 vials. The variance is 12 vials. At a unit cost of ₹850, the variance value is 12 × ₹850 = ₹10,200.

FindingVialsValue
Issued to ICU in emergencies, not yet posted8₹6,800
No document found4₹3,400
Total variance12₹10,200

The eight vials issued to ICU still need to be checked against patient bills; if any were never billed, that is revenue leakage as well as a stock error. The four undocumented vials are the unexplained variance and should be investigated by item, location, shift and user. If the same item shows unexplained variance in the next audit, treat it as a control issue, not a counting error.

Sampling and frequency

Counting every item every month is hard in a large pharmacy. A practical approach is to count all high-value, controlled and high-variance items in full, and sample the rest. ABC VED analysis helps decide which items deserve full counts. Rotate the sample so every item is counted over the year, and increase frequency for locations or items with repeated variance.

After the audit: report and actions

  1. Summarise variance by location, item category and value, separating explained and unexplained variance.
  2. Post approved adjustments with reasons and approvers; do not adjust stock silently.
  3. Raise billing corrections for issues that were dispensed but not billed, where still possible.
  4. Assign an owner and due date for each finding, such as a register gap or a storage issue.
  5. Review the same findings in the next audit to confirm they are closed.

Share the audit summary with the pharmacy head, finance and operations. Pharmacy variance often points to process gaps in wards, OT or billing, not only in the pharmacy itself. The hospital revenue leakage checklist covers related checks outside the pharmacy.
