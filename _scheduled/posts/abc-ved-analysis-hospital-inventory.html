ABC VED analysis is a hospital inventory method that combines consumption value with clinical criticality. ABC ranks items by annual value, while VED ranks them as Vital, Essential or Desirable, so pharmacy teams know which items need the tightest control and which items must never stock out. It is one of the most practical ways to manage medicines, consumables and stores without treating every item equally.

Hospital inventory has a built-in conflict. Finance wants lower stock and fewer write-offs. Clinicians want immediate availability. Pharmacy wants fewer emergency purchases. Patients expect medicines and consumables to be available when prescribed. ABC VED analysis creates a common language for these trade-offs.

ABC analysis in hospital pharmacy

ABC analysis classifies items by annual consumption value. Annual consumption value is usually calculated as annual quantity consumed multiplied by unit cost. Items are then sorted from highest to lowest value. A items consume the largest share of money, B items are moderate and C items are low value.

Annual consumption value = Annual quantity consumed × Unit purchase cost

The exact cut-offs can vary. Many hospitals use indicative bands such as A for the highest-value items, B for the next group and C for the large number of low-value items. The point is not the percentage itself; it is to focus management attention where money is concentrated. ABC should be based on actual consumption, not only purchase value, because one bulk purchase can otherwise distort the classification.

VED analysis in hospital inventory

VED analysis classifies inventory by criticality. Vital items are essential for life-saving or emergency care and should not stock out. Essential items are important for routine care but may have some substitutes or manageable delay. Desirable items are useful but less critical.

VED should not be decided by purchase value alone. A low-cost emergency medicine can be vital. A high-cost elective implant may be financially important but not required on every shelf. Clinical input is necessary, especially from pharmacy, nursing, emergency, ICU, OT and speciality heads.

VED classMeaningInventory priority
VitalStock-out may affect urgent or critical careHigh availability, strict monitoring
EssentialNeeded for common care pathwaysPlanned reorder and substitutes
DesirableUseful but less criticalLower stock, periodic review

ABC is financial; VED is clinical

A hospital needs both views. A low-cost vital drug cannot be ignored just because it is C class, and a costly desirable item should not be overstocked just because it is A class.

ABC VED matrix: how to combine both

After assigning ABC and VED classes, combine them into a matrix. AV items are high-value and vital, so they need senior attention. CV items may be low-value but clinically critical, so stock-out prevention matters more than cost reduction. AD items are high-value but desirable, so purchase approval and stock limits should be strict.

This matrix supports practical decisions: who approves purchase, how often stock is reviewed, which items get cycle counting, and where expiry alerts should be stricter. It also helps resolve disagreements between finance and clinical teams because the discussion moves from opinion to agreed categories.

How ABC VED improves reorder levels

Reorder levels should not be copied across all items. AV items need strong monitoring, approved suppliers and clear minimum stock. CV items need availability despite low value. AD and CD items should not consume shelf space unless there is a clear use case. This helps reduce both stock-outs and overstock.

  • AV items: Keep reliable minimum stock, review frequently and monitor expiry closely.
  • AE items: Link purchase to consumption and planned procedures.
  • AD items: Require approval, because high-value desirable stock can become dead stock.
  • CV items: Protect from stock-out even if value is low.
  • CD items: Consider low stock, substitute use or indent-based purchase.

These controls should feed into pharmacy expiry management. High-value slow-moving items are often where write-offs hurt most.

ABC VED and pharmacy reconciliation

Classification is useful only when stock records are accurate. If physical quantity, system quantity and billing quantity do not match, reorder decisions will be wrong. A pharmacy may reorder an item that exists physically but was not entered correctly, or assume stock exists when it has already been consumed.

That is why ABC VED should be paired with pharmacy inventory reconciliation. AV and CV items should be counted more frequently than low-risk items. Any unexplained variance in vital or high-value stock should be escalated quickly. This also supports broader hospital revenue leakage controls and pharmacy pilferage prevention.

Common mistakes in ABC VED analysis

The most common mistake is doing the analysis once and never updating it. Consumption changes when doctors change, specialities grow, packages change or new protocols are introduced. Another mistake is allowing purchase teams to decide VED alone. Criticality requires clinical input.

A second mistake is making the matrix too academic. The output should change daily controls: reorder quantity, approval level, cycle count frequency, expiry review and vendor negotiation. If nothing changes after classification, the exercise has become paperwork.

  1. Using purchase quantity instead of actual consumption.
  2. Ignoring stock in wards, OT, ICU and emergency.
  3. Classifying brand-wise when molecule-level review is needed, or the reverse.
  4. Not considering substitutes and formulary decisions.
  5. Failing to link classification with approval, reorder and audit frequency.
  6. Not reviewing changes after new consultants or service lines are added.

ABC VED also supports wider hospital KPIs because inventory days, stock-outs, expiry value and purchase variance all affect operational performance.

Governance and review rhythm

A practical review rhythm is monthly for exceptions and quarterly for full classification. Pharmacy can prepare data, finance can review value, and clinical representatives can approve VED changes. For hospital groups, the same item may have different VED classes by facility depending on services offered, so classification should not be blindly copied.

For example, an obstetric emergency medicine may be vital in a maternity hospital but not stocked in the same way at a diagnostic centre. Classification should follow the service profile, not only the central purchase catalogue.

Hospitals should document who can override reorder rules, who approves emergency purchase and how substitutions are handled. For regulated medicines, cold-chain products or controlled items, teams should check current official guidance and internal policy before changing storage or purchase rules.
