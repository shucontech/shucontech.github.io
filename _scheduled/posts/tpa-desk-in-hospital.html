A TPA desk in a hospital is the insurance help desk that manages cashless treatment for insured patients: it verifies the policy, sends pre-authorisation and enhancement requests to the insurer or TPA, answers their queries and obtains final approval before discharge. TPA stands for Third Party Administrator, an intermediary licensed by IRDAI that processes health insurance claims on behalf of insurers. Most hospitals use "TPA desk" and "insurance desk" to mean the same team.

For hospital administrators, the TPA desk sits at an awkward junction. It depends on doctors for clinical notes, on billing for running bills, on the front office for patient details and on payers for decisions it does not control. When the desk runs well, insured patients barely notice it. When it runs badly, it becomes the main reason for discharge delays, claim deductions and long-pending receivables.

TPA full form in hospital and medical insurance

The TPA full form is Third Party Administrator. In medical insurance, the "first party" is the insured person and the "second party" is the insurance company. The TPA is a third party appointed by the insurer to service claims: issuing health cards, handling cashless requests from network hospitals, scrutinising documents and processing claims.

Not every insurer uses a TPA. Some insurers process cashless requests and claims through their own in-house claims teams. From the hospital's side, the workflow looks similar either way, which is why the team is often simply called the insurance desk. The rules on who may act as a TPA and what they may do are set by IRDAI, and hospitals should check payer communications and agreements for the current process rather than relying on old practice.

Roles and responsibilities of the TPA desk

A TPA desk is a coordination function, not a clerical one. Its job is to make sure that every insured patient's file is complete, consistent and defensible from the first request to the last payment. Typical responsibilities include:

  • verifying policy status, sum insured, room rent eligibility, co-pay and known exclusions at admission;
  • preparing and submitting pre-authorisation requests with the doctor's notes and estimate;
  • tracking approvals, queries and denials for every open case;
  • raising enhancement requests when stay, procedure or bill value changes;
  • explaining patient-payable amounts, such as non-payable items and co-pay, to families early;
  • compiling the final bill, discharge summary and supporting documents for final approval;
  • dispatching or uploading claim files after discharge and tracking settlement and deductions.

In smaller hospitals one executive may do all of this. Larger hospitals often split the desk into admission-side and discharge-side roles, with a desk in-charge who owns payer relationships, escalations and reporting. Some hospitals add a medical officer or claims coordinator who reviews clinical justification before files go out, which can reduce avoidable queries.

Daily workflow of a hospital TPA desk

The TPA desk workflow follows the patient's stay. Each stage has a trigger, an output and a risk if it is done late.

1. Admission and policy verification

When an insured patient is advised admission, the desk checks the policy or card, confirms the patient's identity against it and notes room rent limits, co-pay and sub-limits that will affect the bill. This is the right moment to tell the family which items are usually patient-payable. The admission process in hospital works far better when payer checks happen before bed allocation, not after.

2. Pre-authorisation

The desk prepares the pre-authorisation request with diagnosis, planned treatment, expected stay, estimate and supporting reports, then submits it on the payer's portal or the agreed channel. Weak clinical notes and estimates that do not match the planned procedure are common reasons for queries. The detailed checklist is covered in the guide to pre-authorisation for cashless hospitalisation.

3. Enhancements during the stay

If the stay extends, the patient moves to ICU, a procedure changes or high-value items are added, the approved amount may no longer cover the bill. The desk should watch the running bill daily and request enhancement with updated clinical notes before the gap becomes large. Waiting until discharge day is one of the most common causes of long discharge delays for insured patients.

4. Discharge and final approval

Once discharge is likely, the desk needs the final bill, discharge summary, investigation reports, implant details where relevant and any other documents the payer asks for. A clean discharge summary format that matches the bill and the pre-authorisation reduces queries at this stage. The patient leaves after final approval and payment of any patient-payable amount.

5. Query handling

Queries can arrive at pre-authorisation, enhancement, final approval or post-discharge scrutiny. Each query should have an owner, a due time and a record of what was sent. Queries that need a doctor's clarification should go to the doctor with the specific question, not a forwarded email chain.

6. Claim submission and settlement tracking

After discharge, the desk dispatches or uploads the claim file within the payer's timelines and tracks settlement. When payment arrives, the desk or finance team should compare it with the approved amount, record any deduction with its reason and follow up where the deduction looks incorrect. This closes the loop with claim deductions in hospital bills and AR days.

Worked example: one cashless case through the desk

The figures below are illustrative. A patient is admitted for surgery with an estimate of ₹1,40,000. The payer gives an initial approval of ₹1,00,000. On day four the running bill reaches ₹1,52,000 because of an extended stay, so the desk raises an enhancement of ₹52,000 with updated notes, and it is approved.

If the desk had not raised the enhancement on day four, the entire ₹54,000 gap would have surfaced on discharge day, with the family waiting while the request was processed. The example shows why the desk must track the running bill, not just the approval letter.

Documents the TPA desk handles

Exact requirements differ by payer, policy and case type, so the desk should maintain a checklist per payer and per common procedure. Commonly requested documents include:

  • insurance card, policy number or e-card, and patient photo ID;
  • doctor's admission advice with provisional diagnosis and planned treatment;
  • cost estimate and expected length of stay;
  • investigation reports that support the diagnosis;
  • clinical notes for enhancements and changes in treatment;
  • final itemised bill, discharge summary and payment receipts;
  • implant invoices or stickers, OT notes and histopathology where relevant.

Keep one file, one truth

Patient name, age, dates, diagnosis and procedure should read the same on the pre-authorisation, the bill and the discharge summary. Many queries are triggered by small mismatches rather than clinical doubt.

TPA desk KPIs to track

A TPA desk should be measured on speed, completeness and money recovered, with each metric split by payer. Useful measures include:

  • Pre-authorisation TAT: time from admission advice to approval.
  • Query rate and query ageing: how many files get queries and how long they stay open.
  • Final approval TAT: time from discharge decision to final approval, a key part of discharge TAT.
  • Claim submission time: days from discharge to file dispatch or upload.
  • Deduction amount and reasons: approved amount minus settled amount, grouped by cause.
  • Denial rate and clean claim rate: see clean claim rate and denial rate formulas.
  • Settlement ageing: outstanding insurance receivables by payer and age bucket.

Set internal targets from your own baseline. Payer behaviour, case mix and documentation requirements vary enough that a target copied from another hospital can be misleading.

Common TPA desk mistakes

  • Checking the policy after admission, so room rent limits and co-pay surprise the family later.
  • Sending pre-authorisation with a generic diagnosis and no clinical justification.
  • Raising enhancements only at discharge instead of when the running bill crosses the approval.
  • Tracking cases in personal email, WhatsApp chats or registers that nobody else can see.
  • Treating deductions as final without checking the reason against the policy and tariff.
  • No handover between shifts, so queries wait overnight without an owner.

Many of these overlap with the TPA claim rejection reasons hospitals see most often.

TPA desk SOP tips

A short written SOP makes the desk less dependent on one experienced executive. It should cover:

  1. When and how payer verification happens for planned and emergency admissions.
  2. Standard document checklists by payer and by common procedure.
  3. A running-bill review rule, for example a daily check against the approved amount.
  4. Query ownership, response deadlines and escalation to the treating doctor or desk in-charge.
  5. Discharge-day sequence: bill freeze, summary check, final approval request, patient counselling.
  6. Post-discharge submission timelines and settlement reconciliation steps.
  7. A daily review of open cases and a weekly review of deductions and ageing by payer.
